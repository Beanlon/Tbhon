Introduction
1.1 Background of the Study
Tuberculosis (TB) is one of the most serious public health issues, particularly in the modern world, for many people in low- and middle-income countries lack access to formal diagnostic services regularly. The bacteria Mycobacterium tuberculosis continues to cause a large number of morbidities and mortalities all over the world. The Philippines is also always listed among the countries with the greatest TB burden. Delays in detection contribute to continued transmission, worsen clinical outcomes, and increase mortality rates; however, conventional confirmatory techniques - chest radiography, sputum smear microscopy, and molecular testing (e.g., GeneXpert), all of which rely on having sufficient laboratory facilities, appropriately trained personnel, and operational funding - cannot be uniformly relied upon by those living in remote/rural or under-resourced areas.
1.1 Background of the Study
Although active TB public health programs continue to be implemented, there remains a number of gaps in the early detection of TB in resource-limited settings:
Persistent delays in identifying presumptive TB cases due to accessibility barriers and limitations in existing screening practices.
Healthcare worker workload and resource constraints that reduce the efficiency of preliminary TB screening activities.
Insufficient screening support mechanisms capable of identifying potentially overlooked TB cases prior to confirmatory diagnosis.
Limited availability of integrated, low-cost, AI-assisted platforms for multimodal TB pre-screening and risk assessment.
Therefore, this study aims to develop a multimodal support system for TB pre-screening using a designated IoT device which is able to perform preliminary risk assessment and decision-support recommendations, using audio classification of cough signals and microscopic image classification of sputum smears, with the help of a low-cost hardware rig and a smartphone application. Specifically, the study seeks to answer the following questions.
1.3 Objectives of the Study
The primary objective of this study is to develop TBhon, an IoT-enabled, mobile-based diagnostic support system for tuberculosis pre-screening that utilizes machine learning and deep learning to analyze respiratory audio and microscopic sputum images, providing a multi-modal assessment of tuberculosis risk. This will be judged on the following:
Develop the IoT acquisition hardware, including the design of an ESP32-based module for respiratory audio capture and an integrated ESP32-OV5640 microscopic imaging module for sputum smear capture.
Curate and preprocess data, utilizing publicly available tuberculosis datasets, and apply signal processing and image feature extraction techniques to ensure model robustness and consistency.
Develop and optimize machine learning models using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to classify TB-related patterns in respiratory audio via Mel-spectrograms and identify visual indicators in Ziehl–Neelsen stained sputum smears.
Implement an integrated multi-modal risk scoring system that fuses acoustic and visual classification outputs to generate real-time risk stratification levels based on multimodal classification confidence and fusion outputs.
Establish a robust communication architecture that facilitates seamless, real-time wireless data transmission between the embedded acquisition modules, edge-processing nodes, and the backend diagnostic server.
Evaluate the classification performance of the machine learning models using standard metrics:
Accuracy;
Precision;
Recall;
F1-Score; and
Classification Response Time.
Assess software quality compliance based on the ISO/IEC 25010 standard (Functional Suitability, Performance Efficiency, Usability, Reliability, and Security); and
Conduct User Acceptance Testing (UAAT) with professionals to ensure validity and operational reliability.
1.4 Significance of the Study
The study helps the following stakeholders:
At risk populations and individuals. TBhon can be utilized as an early warning device for potential TB risk, prompting early consultation which helps minimize the transmission of TB in the household and community.
Community health workers and primary health care units. The system can be used as a triage tool to determine those people who require confirmatory test, particularly when laboratory access is limited.
Healthcare system and public health programmes. The system may help to lower the cost of TB pre-screening and potentially expand the use of screening in regions which complement national TB elimination programmes, like the Philippine Strategic TB Elimination Plan.
Researchers and developers. The study forms part of a suite of working reference designs for multimodal (audio + image) ML pipelines that can be used on low-cost embedded devices, including those that capture images using microscopy, which will inform ongoing and future work in mobile health and AI-aided screening.
Academic institutions and students. In the study, a combination of embedded systems, computer vision, sound machine learning and mobile application development are combined in a healthcare context, making this an excellent practical guide.
1.5 Scope and Limitation
The study was targeted at the development of an IoT-based multimodal tuberculosis (TB) pre-screening diagnostic support system, TBhon, that classifies the risk of TB in its initial stages with audio inputs from respiratory cough images from a microscopic smear of sputum. It features embedded sensing, wireless communication, edge computing, and machine learning, enabling TB risk assessment in a portable, non-invasive way in community settings.
The architecture of the system incorporates an ESP32 microcontroller, which is used to read the audio of the cough and an I2S MEMS microphone. An OV5640 camera module is mounted on a compound microscope to capture the images of the sputum smear. Data collected are sent to a FastAPI server via Wi-Fi where they are processed by Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) models deployed on a Raspberry Pi 4 edge-computing platform. A multimodal TB risk classification, Low Risk, Moderate Risk or High Risk, is determined by a probability mechanism with fusion.
This study is centered on the two modalities, respiratory cough audio modality, and the sputum smear microscopy images. The imaging of X-ray, molecular testing using GeneXpert, and the integration of laboratory culture analysis and hospital information systems is not covered. The system also is limited in its ability to make preliminary diagnosis, suggest treatment options and monitor patients, and cannot carry out clinical diagnosis, treatment recommendation, and patient monitoring.
In the study, the model training and evaluation were done with public cough audio and sputum smear image database. Variability of the datasets, class imbalance, and variability in the environment of the audio acquisition, inconsistencies of image quality and light in the microscopy limit the performance of the system. Moreover, the computational power of the ESP32 and Raspberry Pi hardware platforms in the prototype implementation limit the system performance and scalability.
Moreover, due to the constraints of prototype development for such IoT systems, budget and time constraints, the experimental prototype of the study was built with the use of readily available embedded components as well as publicly available datasets and was of a small size. Thus, no large-scale clinical validation, no deployment in the field, and no long-term real-world testing in different healthcare settings were done. This may affect the overall and diverse use of the system.
Despite the various limitations discussed, the study provides a general framework leveraging embedded sensing, edge computing and machine learning in a single portable architecture to pre-screen TB.
1.6 Algorithm Concepts
The proposed TBhon system is developed by implementing a multimodal machine learning approach with three main algorithmic components: cough audio classification, sputum smear image classification, and multimodal risk fusion. These algorithms are used to produce a preliminary tuberculosis risk level (Low, Moderate, or High Risk).
The first algorithm is a cough audio classification algorithm. The system is designed to monitor cough audio by connecting the ESP32 microcontroller with an I2S MEMS microphone. The acoustic cough signal is preprocessed by noise reduction, trimming, and standardization. This sound is then transformed into a Mel spectrogram—showing how the frequency of coughs change with time. This spectrogram serves as the input for a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model that classifies the cough pattern and gives the audio-based tuberculosis probability (Paudio).
The second algorithm is the classification algorithm of the sputum smear image. The microscopic images of the sputum smear are acquired by the system that comprises an OV2640 camera module attached to a compound microscope. The captured image is pre-processed by resizing, normalizing, and image enhancing. A CNN model is then used to extract visual features from the image of the sputum smear and classify the image according to the indicators of AFB or abnormal smear patterns.
The result of this model is an image-based probability of tuberculosis which is referred to as Pimage. The multimodal fusion algorithm is the third algorithm. The probability is obtained by summing the probabilities of the audio model and the image model weighted by a soft combination function: TB_risk = (w1 × Paudio) + (w2 × Pimage) In this equation, w1 is the weight of the cough audio model and w2 is the weight of the sputum smear image model.
TB_risk = (w1 × Paudio) + (w2 × Pimage)
In this equation, w1 is the weight of the cough audio model, and w2 is the weight of the sputum smear image model. This study provides an evaluation, and the audio model of the cough could be slightly more important since it had better performance in testing. Lastly, the algorithm to classify the decision processes the computed TB_risk score to a final risk category. A score of less than 0.33 is considered a Low-Risk score. If the score is in the range of 0.33 to less than 0.66, then the result is considered a Moderate Risk. A score of 0.66 or above is considered to be High Risk.
If TB_risk < 0.33 → Low Risk
If 0.33 ≤ TB_risk < 0.66 → Moderate Risk
If TB_risk ≥ 0.66 → High Risk
In brief, the algorithm idea behind TBhon is a fusion of two classifiers, namely, a cough audio classifier, and a sputum smear image classifier, based on CNN. The outputs of these are combined into one probability score that is then mapped to a preliminary TB risk level to be displayed in the mobile application.
RELATED WORKS
In healthcare, the adoption of the Internet of Things (IoT) has paved the way for the creation of IoT-based intelligent respiratory monitoring and tuberculosis (TB) screening systems that can gather data in real-time, remotely monitor patients, and analyze data automatically. Typical IoT healthcare ecosystems include a variety of interconnected sensors and embedded microcontrollers, wireless communication modules, cloud or edge processing systems, and intelligent analytics frameworks, all working together to enable healthcare decision-making and patient monitoring.
Naufal et al. created an artificial intelligence based portable prototype for early diagnosis of tuberculosis by breath analysis which integrates with the internet of things (IoT) technologies [1]. The approach relied on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller paired with a metal oxide semiconductor (MOS) gas-sensor array to detect chemical signatures in the breath related to TB infection. Feature reduction was done using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and classification was performed using machine learning algorithms including Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest, Gradient Boosting and Artificial Neural Network (ANN). The study showed that embedding sensing technologies, wireless communication, and AI-driven analysis in a portable screening platform for TB is possible.
Likewise, Sharma et al. suggested an IoT solution that monitors patients and detects adverse drug reactions to enhance treatment adherence by TB patients [2]. They used a microcontroller called NodeMCU (ESP8266) which is a wireless module, and they built their own system to constantly track patient conditions with temperature, pulse, cough, and laboratory-value sensors. The system facilitated remote monitoring of healthcare parameters and automated symptom tracking through rule-based evaluation and integration with the IoT server. The study highlighted the importance of IoT ecosystems in the provision of healthcare services beyond traditional healthcare settings, with the ability to monitor and communicate with a patient's physiology on an ongoing basis.
In the context of healthcare IoT systems, the sensing layer is one of the most critical components in respiratory disease monitoring systems, where physiological signals need to be continually captured and processed. Embedded microcontrollers and hand-held sensors are now being increasingly used to monitor respiratory behavior, airflow patterns, and pulmonary function, according to recent studies.
Lavanya designed a respiratory monitoring system with the help of microcontroller, Arduino UNO, and a sensor which was based on thermistor which was used to monitor the patient's breathing for a continuous period [5]. The system worked by measuring the periodic temperature variations produced during the inhalation and exhalation cycles to determine the respiratory rates and using threshold-based algorithms to identify abnormal respiratory activity. The study showed that the lightweight embedded system could successfully be used to monitor the respiration in the real-time environment of portable healthcare apparatus.
Likewise, Simić et al. developed a portable respiration monitoring system based on capacitive facemask sensor which were embroidered and attached to a portable system based on microcontroller [7]. The system was based around signal processing techniques to analyse the changes in airflow and chest motion to estimate breathing patterns and respiratory rates. The non-invasive and wearable nature of the device showcased the growing need for portable respiratory sensing technologies in healthcare applications.
Azzahra et al. later designed an Arduino Mega-based portable peak expiratory flow rate measurement system that incorporates an MPX5100GP pressure sensor [8]. The system was deployed to convert pressure measurements to PFM to evaluate lung health and pulmonary function in respiratory diseases. The results obtained from their work demonstrated the potential of embedded sensors and microcontroller platforms in respiratory monitoring and evaluation.
The analytical capabilities of IoT health systems have been greatly enhanced with recent developments in edge computing and AI. By moving AI-powered decisions and analytics to the edge, Edge Computing reduces reliance on centralized cloud platforms, improves portability, rapid response time, and real-time monitoring.
Rivas-Navarrete et al. [9] have proposed an edge computing system for automatic detection of chronic respiratory diseases, based on audio analysis. The system was based on the use of smartphones and raspberry pi devices, and the processing of the audio signals from the breathing was done locally. Respiratory sounds were analysed using Machine Learning techniques, such as Mel Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) and chromagram feature extraction, with the aim of classifying respiratory conditions. The research showed that edge processing is a strong prospect to enhance real-time respiratory condition analysis without the need for cloud services.
Likewise, Suda investigated the detection of tuberculosis using audio analysis of coughs by a ML based machine learning application on a smart phone [10]. Two-dimensional Convolutional Neural Network (2D-CNN) and XGBoost were used as the ensemble system to classify TB with mel-spectrogram representations of cough sounds. The findings showed that respiratory sound analysis using AI can be used as a non-invasive method and is easily accessible for initial screening for TB.
Paul et al. also demonstrated the possibility of using Edge AI for the monitoring of respiratory diseases by creating a device to predict whooping cough using an Arduino Nano RP2040 microcontroller [11]. The system will feature a light-weight CNN that will be integrated to process the respiratory-related signals directly in embedded devices. The research highlighted the potential for using intelligent artificial intelligence (AI) models on microcontroller devices with limited computational power to accurately predict respiratory diseases in real-time and monitor health conditions.
The role of AI in the detection and diagnostic improvement of TB in different health care systems is becoming increasingly important. In the medical imaging domain, deep learning and machine learning have already found applications in numerous aspects related to automatic disease screening, diagnostic support in respiratory signal analysis, and physiological data understanding.
Sigit et al. designed a motorized microscope system using YOLOv8 object detection algorithm for automated detection of TB bacteria [12]. The deep learning-based image processing techniques were used to automatically localize TB bacilli in the sputum smear images. The study indicated that the AI microscopy systems can support laboratory diagnosis of TB by automatically identifying and categorizing the objects from the microscopy image.
In the meantime, Hansun et al. have carried out a systematic evaluation of the tuberculosis detection approaches based on AI, where they determined the wide use of deep learning architectures like VGG-16, ResNet-50, and DenseNet-121 on radiographical, molecular, and physiological datasets [15]. Their review pointed to improved diagnostic accuracy and effectiveness of AI models for TB screening and analysis of respiratory diseases.
The literature examined shows that IoT healthcare ecosystems are designed with embedded sensing devices, microcontroller platforms, physiological monitoring technologies, edge computing frameworks, wireless communication systems and artificial intelligence algorithms in order to facilitate intelligent respiratory healthcare applications. Previous research has successfully integrated respiratory sensing, audio analysis of coughs, AI support for diagnostics, and remote healthcare monitoring into portable healthcare systems.
III. MATERIALS AND METHODS
This section describes the tools, techniques, and methods that will be used in this study. The procedures for evaluation of data and results will be discussed in this section.
3.1 System Development Methodology
Figure I. CRISP-DM TBhon Diagram
The first phase, Business Understanding, defines the primary objective of the system, which is to develop a mobile-based TB pre-screening diagnostic support tool capable of identifying potential tuberculosis risk using cough audio and sputum images. This phase establishes the problem context of delayed TB detection in low-resource communities and guides the system requirements for accessible and early-stage screening.
The second phase, Data Understanding, involves gathering and analyzing relevant datasets such as cough recordings, sputum smear images, and supporting medical references. This stage focuses on identifying patterns and characteristics associated with TB indicators, as well as understanding the structure, quality, and limitations of available data.
The third phase, Data Preparation, involves cleaning and transforming raw audio and image data into machine-learning-ready formats. This includes audio preprocessing such as Mel-spectrogram conversion using Torchaudio, and image preprocessing techniques such as resizing, normalization, and enhancement to improve feature quality and model performance.
The fourth phase, Modeling, focuses on the development and training of machine learning models using PyTorch. CNN-based architectures are applied separately for cough audio and sputum image classification, with iterative training used to optimize accuracy and improve generalization due to variability in respiratory sounds and image conditions.
The fifth phase, Evaluation, assesses model performance using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. This ensures that both audio and image classifiers produce reliable predictions before integration into the final system. Validation tests are also conducted to verify consistency and robustness of the TB risk classification outputs.
The final phase, Deployment, involves integrating the trained models into a mobile-accessible application through a FastAPI-based inference system. This enables real-time TB risk prediction based on user inputs. Feedback from users and testing environments is considered for continuous improvement, allowing the system to be refined iteratively, which aligns with the cyclical nature of CRISP-DM.
The closing phase, Iteration and Improvement, ensures consistency with CRISP-DM principles. This phase assesses that the system supports iterative refinement based on model performance evaluation and testing feedback. This allows continuous improvement of classification accuracy and robustness under varying real-world conditions.
3.2 Conceptual Framework
Figure II. Tbhon Screening Framework
The proposed system integrates three main algorithms to process and classify tuberculosis risk. First, the audio classification algorithm takes the cough audio signal as input, applies noise reduction, converts it into a Mel spectrogram, and feeds it into the CNN model to output the probability of TB-related cough (Paudio). The utilization of Mel spectrogram along with CNNs has been already validated as an effective and efficient approach to automated respiratory decease detection from cough noise [16]. Second, the image classification algorithm processes sputum smear images by performing preprocessing (resizing and normalization), followed by feature extraction using the CNN,[13][14] and classification into abnormal or normal indicators, producing an image-based probability (Pimage). Third, a multimodal fusion algorithm combines both outputs using a weighted equation: TB_risk = (w1 × Paudio) + (w2 × Pimage), where w1 and w2 represent the assigned weights for each modality. Finally, a decision algorithm classifies the overall risk level based on the computed TB_risk value: low risk if TB_risk < 0.33, moderate risk if 0.33 ≤ TB_risk < 0.66, and high risk if TB_risk ≥ 0.66. Multimodial fusion is perfect in this scenario as it allows for better accuracy in terms prediction and analysis based on multiple sources of data such as images and audio compared to a unimodal which only uses a single source of data for prediction.[3][4][6]
3.3 System Architecture
Figure III. System Architecture Diagram
The proposed Tuberculosis screening architecture is designed as a multi-level architecture that seamlessly transitions between hardware data acquisition with deep learning-driven risk assessment. As shown in figure V, the system is organized into six functional layers. At the starting point is the IoT Device and Edge Acquisition Layer which are the ones responsible for handling diagnostic data collection via IoT hardware components and modules such as the ESP32 integrated with the I2S MEMS microphone for cough recording, and the OV5640 camera module handles capturing sputum smear images. These functional modules utilize WIFI to transmit data via HTTP and MQTT which is passed through the User layer. This layer is where the frontend of the application sits and where the data reflets so that the user can see the data that they gathered during screening.
While the user sees the UI and the entire process itself on a more visual level, the actual process runs behind the Application Processing layer. This layer validates the incoming information received from the IoT which is then passed unto the Machine Learning Layer, this layer is like the brains of the process as it uses PyTorch-based CNNs to process the data in parallel, the audio evaluates 128-band Mel-spectograms at 16 khz to detect acoustic TB signatures, while the image classifier assesses digitized smears for Acid-Fast Bacilli (AFB) detection based on the ODS1 dataset indicators. The dedicated fusion module handles the overall analysis, calculating a unified percentage that becomes the final verdict for the screening shown as TB risk level categorized as low, moderate, and high as shown in 3.2 conceptual frameworks.
After the screening has been settled screening data are stored in the Data Management Layer, which stores every data captured by the application to the database such as account details, user information, screening data etc. Both the Machine Learning  and the Database Layer are both connected to the Digital Ocean domains where it is seperated to two droplets which is the Backend and the Machine Learning, which are running on specific online servers with their own IP address as to make the functionality migrate from a local entry point to an accessible server for development. Finally, the Output Layer is the overall data/information that are displayed to the user itself that translates the model inferences into readable.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
This section reports the datasets, experimental apparatus, training procedures, and quantitative findings of the TBhon multimodal tuberculosis screening system. Evaluation encompassed unimodal cough and sputum classifiers, cross-fold generalization, architectural ablation, operating-point calibration, and pre-inference quality control. Screening-level risk integration is described qualitatively; a dedicated held-out evaluation of the full fused triage score was not completed and is noted as a limitation in Section IV-G.
Datasets and Experimental Setup
The evaluation employed two publicly available data sources.
The cough audio dataset was drawn from the open Kaggle tuberculosis audio corpus and comprised 7,817 labeled respiratory recordings in total. All recordings were converted to mono, resampled to 16 kHz, and normalized to a fixed temporal window prior to feature extraction. The deployed hybrid classifier used four-second clips and sixty-four Mel frequency bins; supplementary convolutional baselines additionally examined six-second clips with 128 Mel bins. Labels were binary (tuberculosis-positive versus tuberculosis-negative). The corpus was organized into three predefined cross-validation folds, each allocating approximately 5,211 recordings to training and 2,606 to testing. An additional 12% of the training partition was withheld as a validation set through stratified sampling with a fixed random seed, preserving class prevalence while supporting early stopping, threshold selection, and branch blending.
The sputum smear image dataset consisted of 1,438 Ziehl–Neelsen stained microscopy fields curated from open research repositories, partitioned into 1,149 training, 142 validation, and 147 test images (approximately 80% / 10% / 10%). Acid-fast bacillus labels were derived from bounding-box annotations by counting visible bacilli per field. Two prediction tasks were evaluated: a binary formulation (AFB-negative versus AFB-positive) and a four-class AFB load-grade formulation. Binary classification employed 224 × 224-pixel inputs with a ResNet-18 backbone; the compact load-grade network used 160 × 160-pixel inputs.
Prototype acquisition. Field samples were collected using an ESP32-S3 microcontroller paired with an INMP441 I2S MEMS microphone (16 kHz audio, up to ten seconds per event, minimum three seconds in the screening workflow) and a microscope-mounted camera acquiring 640 × 480-pixel JPEG frames. Recordings and images were transmitted over Wi-Fi to a FastAPI inference server for preprocessing and classification.
Compute the environment. Model training and offline evaluation were performed on a workstation with an Intel Core i5 processor, 8 GB RAM, and a CUDA-enabled NVIDIA GPU, using PyTorch 2.x, Librosa, and OpenCV.
Cough Classifier Comparison
Two cough classifiers were compared on held-out test data. The baseline was a convolutional neural network operating on log-Mel spectrograms, trained with AdamW (learning rate 3 × 10⁻⁴), batch size 32, up to thirty epochs, and early stopping after five epochs without validation macro-F1 improvement. Augmentation included temporal shifting, additive noise, and time–frequency masking.
The hybrid model combined a convolutional branch with a gradient-boosted ensemble fed by hand-crafted acoustic descriptors (MFCCs, temporal derivatives, and summary statistics). CNN and ensemble posterior probabilities were fused through validation-tuned weighted averaging. Domain augmentations simulating lossy compression and reverberation were applied during hybrid training.

Training Procedure and Evaluation Matrix
Cough Audio Classification
Two cough classifiers were evaluated.
The baseline model was a convolutional neural network operating on Mel-spectrogram inputs.  Training employed the AdamW optimizer at a learning rate of 3 × 10⁻⁴, a batch size of 32, and up to thirty epochs, with early stopping applied after five consecutive epochs without improvement in validation macro-F1 score. On-the-fly augmentation included temporal shifting, additive Gaussian noise, and time–frequency masking.
The production model was a hybrid architecture combining a convolutional branch with a gradient-boosted ensemble fed by hand-c
rafted acoustic descriptors, including Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients and their temporal derivatives. The convolutional branch used sixty-four Mel frequency bins and four-second clips over a legacy network topology. The convolutional component was trained for eight to twelve epochs; the ensemble comprised three hundred to six hundred decision trees. Posterior probabilities from both branches were combined through validation-tuned weighted averaging prior to thresholding. Additional augmentation simulated lossy compression and room reverberation to improve robustness to field recording conditions.
Sputum Smear Classification
The binary AFB classifier employed a ResNet-18 backbone pretrained on ImageNet, fine-tuned for thirty epochs with AdamW at a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻³, batch size 32, and class-weighted cross-entropy to mitigate label imbalance. Training-time augmentation included random resized cropping, horizontal and vertical flipping, in-plane rotation, and color jitter. The decision threshold on the positive-class probability was selected on the validation set to maximize macro-F1 score. A complementary four-class load-grade classifier used a lightweight custom convolutional architecture and was evaluated under the same train–validation–test partition.

Evaluation Metrics
Classification performance was quantified using accuracy, precision, recall, and macro-averaged F1-score on the held-out test partitions. Macro-averaging treated all classes equally and was preferred where class prevalence was skewed. A fixed random seed was employed to ensure reproducibility of validation splits and training initialization. End-to-end response time was not systematically measured and is not reported herein.

CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATION
Conclusion
The study successfully developed TBhon, an IoT-enabled mobile-based multimodal tuberculosis pre-screening and triage support system integrating a React Native mobile application, a cloud-hosted backend, machine learning inference services, and optional ESP32-S3 IoT acquisition hardware. By combining symptom assessment, cough audio analysis, sputum smear image analysis, and weighted multimodal risk fusion, the system provides Low, Moderate, and High triage recommendations intended to support community health workers during TB pre-screening while emphasizing that it is not a replacement for clinical diagnosis.
The proposed system achieved the study objectives by implementing IoT-assisted respiratory audio and sputum image acquisition, developing and deploying machine learning models for cough and sputum analysis, integrating cloud-based communication and inference services, and implementing an interpretable multimodal risk-scoring framework. Functional validation demonstrated successful end-to-end operation across supported mobile devices, while software quality assessment based on ISO/IEC 25010 and professional user acceptance testing indicated high levels of functional suitability, usability, security, and overall user satisfaction. These findings demonstrate the technical feasibility and practical usability of the integrated prototype within booth-operated and community-based TB screening workflows.
Although the developed models achieved performance appropriate for prototype pre-screening applications, the system remains intended solely as a decision-support tool. Evaluation was conducted using publicly available datasets and controlled prototype deployment conditions rather than prospective clinical studies. Consequently, the reported results demonstrate engineering feasibility and user acceptability rather than clinical effectiveness. Additional validation using larger and more diverse local populations, particularly in rural and resource-limited settings, is necessary before wider deployment.
Overall, the study demonstrates that integrating multimodal artificial intelligence, mobile computing, cloud infrastructure, and IoT-assisted data acquisition can provide a practical and accessible approach for supporting early tuberculosis pre-screening and referral. The proposed TBhon system establishes a foundation for future research on AI-assisted public health screening technologies that complement existing diagnostic pathways and contribute to earlier identification and more organized TB screening initiatives.
Recommendation
First, future developers should implement offline or low-bandwidth operations with local request queuing and synchronization when connectivity is restored. UAT evaluators consistently identified unstable Wi-Fi and rural network conditions as the primary limitation (Item 20, weighted mean 3.83), and this enhancement would improve reliability for barangay health stations and remote screening booths.
Second, the mobile application should include a structured onboarding tutorial, illustrated capture guidance, and clearer ML processing status indicators for first-time booth staff. Evaluators from clinical and medical laboratory science backgrounds noted that non-technical users require additional instructional support before independent operation.
Third, referral outputs should integrate location-based mapping to the nearest accredited TB DOTS clinic, RHU, or GeneXpert-capable facility. Clinical evaluators recommended that triage results explicitly link patients to geographically relevant follow-up pathways rather than generic referral text alone.
Fourth, the cough ML pipeline should be expanded with additional training data including female cough recordings, diverse acoustic environments, and improved quality-gate feedback that highlights rejected segments rather than full replay loops to improve TB-class recall and user trust in audio-based triage signals.
Fifth, the sputum analysis module should be enhanced with on-screen AFB bounding-box visualization, expanded AFB-negative training samples, and prospective field validation using institution-approved clinical smear images to strengthen specificity estimates beyond the limited six-sample negative test partition.
Sixth, the symptom checklist may be extended with additional clinical risk factors suggested during UAT such as comorbid conditions, smoking exposure, and allergy-related cough differentiators while preserving a concise staff-operated workflow suitable for booth throughput.
Seventh, IoT deployment should be completed through native or EAS builds enabling full BLE Wi-Fi provisioning, on-device status LEDs or display feedback, and field troubleshooting guides to reduce setup friction for ESP32-S3 cough and sputum capture modules.
Eighth, a multi-site field pilot with LGU, RHU, or NGO partners should be conducted to measure real screening volume, referral follow-through, cloud inference latency under production load, and long-term model drift using logged prediction data from the deployed FastAPI service.
Ninth, future researchers may integrate supplementary modalities such as chest X-ray CAD interfaces or GeneXpert result logging as optional triage inputs only, maintaining honest pre-screening scope and avoiding presentation of the fused risk score as a standalone diagnostic conclusion.
Finally, multilingual support (e.g., Filipino and Cebuano/Bisaya) for checklist items, learn content, result counseling, and disclaimers is recommended to improve accessibility for diverse clients in Mindanao screening environments and to align with community-oriented tuberculosis elimination initiatives.
The researchers strongly recommend the continuous exploration and integration of emerging technologies within community TB pre-screening and public health monitoring environments to improve multimodal triage efficiency, booth-operated screening capability, and technology-assisted tuberculosis elimination sustainability in resource-limited settings.

CHAPTER 1 - INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background of the Study
Tuberculosis (TB) is a contagious bacterial infection that affects the lungs of the person; this is caused by Mycobacterium Tuberculosis. It spreads through the lungs of the person that is infected and can be contagious when the sick host coughs or sneezes. This sickness can spread airborne. Early symptoms such as persistent, wheezing cough, chest pain, and sputum production are often ignored or misdiagnosed as common cough.
In the last year this disease has claimed over 1.2 million lives and has affected an estimated 10.7 million people. This disease is a major risk concern for the entire world as 87% of cases occur in just 30 countries, top contributors being India, Indonesia, Philippines, China and many more.  Despite the advancement of diagnosis, treatment and innovations, millions are still affected because of this disease (WHO Global TB report 2025, November 2025). Continuous effort has been placed under this global risk as many of these countries continue to make strides to lessen the burden of this disease. In the Philippines, the Department of Health (DOH) and the World Health Organization (WHO) have collaborated and are intensifying efforts to eliminate Tuberculosis (TB) in the country with a goal of screening 12 million Filipinos by 2026 under the Philippine Strategic TB Elimination Plan Phase 2 starting from 2025 to 2030. The government led by Ferdinand Marcos Jr., is increasing the support for TB programs, including a proposed 4.2 million budget for 2026, which is nearly double the previous year and with the help of advanced technologies like AI-powered chest X-rays and rapid molecular tests to improve the screening of Tuberculosis (Montemayor, 2025).
With the increased development of solutions for this disease, a drastic decrease in the population affected will certainly happen, however if left unattended, major consequences will occur especially in a person’s health in the long term. Long term complications of Tuberculosis include permanent lung damage and scarring, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), coughing up blood (hemoptysis), Lung collapse (pneumothorax) and many more.  Even in early treatment there is still at least 2 to 12 percent change of relapse, especially if medication is not completed properly (Lillis, 2024). This massive and reoccurring disease has affected patients worldwide, not only does it affect patients worldwide, but it also affects healthcare workers as they also gain effects of the TB infection. 85 studies of research articles were analyzed spanning from 2005 until 2017 and found that nearly half of the healthcare workers and about one-third of healthcare students tested positive for TB exposure using standard diagnostic tests. Infections rates of tuberculosis were very high in countries with high TB occurrences. Annual LTBI rates were reaching up to 17 to 18 % among healthcare workers (Apriani et al., 2019). Asides from that, it has also affected entire families as it creates a negative impact on socioeconomic status patients as it is unresolvable in times of financial difficulties. As the disease becomes a huge burden to them as it creates an increased risk of “financial crises”, which has caused delayed and disrupted education among children and older children and adults as they experience malnutrition (Marconi et al., 2020).
Therefore, the researchers propose TBhon, a mobile-based tuberculosis (TB) pre-screening system integrated with convolutional neural network (CNN)-based cough audio analysis, smear sputum image classification, multimodal risk prediction, and cloud-based data management technologies. The system utilizes Mel-spectrogram representations of cough recordings, computer vision techniques for sputum smear analysis, and the PyTorch deep learning framework to support automated TB risk assessment. Publicly available and clinically relevant datasets, including TB cough audio datasets and Ziehl–Neelsen stained sputum smear microscopy image datasets, will be used for model training and evaluation. The proposed system aims to provide accessible, non-invasive, and real-time preliminary TB risk screening through a mobile platform, supporting earlier risk identification and encouraging timely medical consultation, particularly in resource-constrained and underserved communities.

These outputs are displayed through the mobile application to support early TB risk identification and encourage timely medical consultation.
1.5 Scope and Limitations of the Study
The study focused on the development of TBhon, a mobile-based machine learning system designed for preliminary tuberculosis (TB) risk classification using respiratory cough audio and smear sputum image inputs. The proposed system was implemented using convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures to process multimodal biomedical data and generate a unified TB risk output categorized as Low, Moderate, or High Risk. The system was designed for mobile deployment to support real-time, non-invasive screening and accessibility in community-based settings.
The scope of the study was limited to two primary data modalities: respiratory audio and sputum smear microscopy images. The respiratory audio module processes cough recordings by converting them into Mel-spectrogram representations for CNN-based feature extraction, while the image module analyzes sputum smear samples to identify visual TB-related indicators such as abnormal coloration and potential blood traces. The system does not include other diagnostic modalities such as chest X-ray imaging, molecular testing (e.g., GeneXpert), or clinical laboratory integration.
The study utilized publicly available datasets for model training and evaluation, including Kaggle-based cough audio datasets and smear sputum microscopy image datasets sourced from research repositories. Dataset variability, quality, and class imbalance may affect model performance and generalization across different real-world environments. Furthermore, the system is dependent on input quality, including background noise in audio recordings and image clarity in sputum samples, which may influence classification accuracy.
The proposed system is limited to binary input processing (audio and image) and does not incorporate real-time clinical data integration, hospital information systems, or electronic health record (EHR) connectivity. It also does not perform medical diagnosis, treatment recommendation, or patient monitoring functions. The output is strictly restricted to probabilistic classification of TB risk levels and is intended solely for preliminary screening support rather than clinical decision-making.
Despite these limitations, the study aims to provide a practical and accessible mobile-based screening tool that leverages machine learning for early TB risk identification, particularly in resource-limited communities where access to diagnostic infrastructure is constrained.

CHAPTER II REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND TECHNOLOGIES
This chapter presents the related literature, studies, and technologies relevant to the development of the proposed tuberculosis TB pre-screening diagnostic support system. The discussion focuses on artificial intelligence, machine learning, computer vision, deep learning, mobile computing, and health informatics as applied to respiratory audio and sputum smear image analysis. It establishes the theoretical and technological foundations supporting the development of TBhon and highlights the research gaps in existing TB detection approaches, particularly in terms of accessibility, multimodal integration, and real-world applicability in resource-limited settings.
2.1 Technological Context of Tuberculosis Screening Systems
Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the most persistent global public health challenges, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to diagnostic infrastructure is limited. According to the World Health Organization (WHO, 2024; WHO, 2025), TB continues to cause millions of infections annually, with significant cases remaining undiagnosed due to limited screening capacity and delayed healthcare access. These challenges highlight the need for accessible, scalable, and technology-driven screening solutions capable of supporting early detection at the community level.
Recent developments in digital health systems and artificial intelligence (AI) have introduced new approaches to TB screening, particularly through automated data analysis and mobile health platforms. AI-based systems enable early risk classification by identifying patterns in clinical and non-clinical data, reducing dependency on traditional laboratory-based diagnostics. However, many of these systems remain isolated in function or require specialized clinical environments, limiting their usability in real-world community settings (Nansamba et al., 2025; Memon et al., 2025).
Furthermore, studies have shown that delays in TB diagnosis are often caused by both system-level and patient-level barriers, including limited awareness, stigma, and poor access to diagnostic services (Preston et al., 2025). These limitations reinforce the need for integrated, mobile-based screening systems that can operate outside hospital environments while maintaining acceptable diagnostic reliability.
2.2 Audio-Based Tuberculosis Detection Systems
Respiratory audio analysis has emerged as a viable non-invasive approach for preliminary TB screening. These systems utilize machine learning techniques to analyze cough sounds and extract discriminative acoustic features associated with pulmonary abnormalities. Typically, cough signals are transformed into Mel-spectrogram representations and processed using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for classification.
Studies such as Akhter (2025) demonstrate the use of dual-stream deep learning architectures for cough-based TB detection, achieving measurable classification accuracy, but showing performance degradation in noisy environments. Similarly, Sharma et al. (2024) developed a ResNet18-based cough classification system (TBscreen), which effectively distinguishes TB-related patterns but is limited to single-modality audio input.
More advanced approaches incorporate multimodal or foundation-model architectures. Suda (2023) introduced a large-scale ensemble model combining CNN and XGBoost for cough classification, achieving high AUROC performance and rapid inference suitable for mobile deployment. Meanwhile, Ma et al. (2025), Buyung and Nugroho (2025), and Memon et al. (2025) further demonstrate that integrating audio with additional data sources improves diagnostic robustness compared to audio-only systems.
Despite these advancements, audio-based TB detection systems remain sensitive to environmental noise, recording quality, and dataset limitations, which reduce their reliability in uncontrolled real-world conditions. This limitation supports the need for complementary diagnostic modalities, such as sputum image analysis, to improve classification stability.
2.3 Sputum and Smear Image-Based TB Detection Systems
Studies show that sputum smear image-based TB detection benefits significantly from computer-aided diagnosis (CADx) systems. Kotei and Thirunavukarasu (2022) found that deep learning models such as CNNs and DCNNs outperform traditional image processing techniques by automatically extracting features from microscopic sputum images, improving accuracy, sensitivity, and F1-score. However, their review also highlights persistent limitations such as small datasets, annotation inconsistencies, and variability in image quality, which affect model generalization.
Related medical imaging studies further support the effectiveness of AI-based TB detection. Flores et al. (2024) report high diagnostic performance using AI-assisted chest X-ray systems aligned with WHO standards but emphasize reliance on costly radiographic infrastructure. Similarly, Connect (2024) notes that portable imaging and CAD tools improve efficiency but still require specialized equipment and trained personnel, limiting deployment in low-resource settings.
Overall, the literature shows that while CADx-based sputum and imaging systems achieve strong performance in controlled environments, their real-world usability remains constrained by infrastructure and dataset limitations. This gap supports the need for accessible, mobile-based solutions, aligning with the proposed TBhon system’s sputum image analysis component for community-level TB screening.
2.4 Multimodal AI Systems for Tuberculosis Detection
Recent research emphasizes that multimodal learning systems significantly improve TB detection performance by combining multiple sources of diagnostic information. Unlike single-modality systems, multimodal frameworks integrate complementary data such as audio signals, imaging results, and clinical information to improve classification robustness.
Ma et al. (2025) demonstrated that combining cough audio with clinical metadata improves TB detection performance, achieving high AUROC values. Similarly, Buyung and Nugroho (2025) showed that foundation-model-based fusion approaches yield improved accuracy and balanced classification metrics. These studies highlight that multimodal integration reduces uncertainty in predictions and enhances diagnostic reliability.
Wainwright et al. (2025) further supports the role of mobile health platforms in TB management, particularly in improving accessibility, patient engagement, and treatment monitoring. However, most existing systems focus on post-diagnosis management rather than early-stage screening.
These findings collectively establish the need for a unified, mobile-based multimodal system capable of performing early TB risk assessment using accessible inputs such as cough audio and sputum images, which directly supports the design direction of TBhon.
2.5 Machine Learning and Computer Vision Foundations
The development of TBhon is grounded in machine learning–based pattern recognition theory, which enables systems to learn discriminative features from complex biomedical data. By leveraging convolutional neural networks (CNNs), researchers can automatically learn hierarchical feature representations from raw data, a capability that makes these architectures highly effective for both audio signal processing and image classification (Mienye &  Swart, 2024).
Computer vision techniques further enhance image-based analysis by enabling automated feature extraction from medical images, reducing the need for manual intervention (Wang et al., 2021). These techniques are essential in processing sputum smear images for TB detection.
Additionally, digital signal processing methods are used to convert respiratory audio into spectrogram-based representations, allowing deep learning models to analyze frequency-domain features. These foundational techniques collectively support the multimodal architecture of TBhon, which integrates audio and image classification pipelines into a unified prediction system.
2.6 Synthesis of Reviewed Literature and Technologies
The reviewed literature and technologies show that tuberculosis (TB) detection has advanced significantly through artificial intelligence, deep learning, and computer vision applications, particularly in cough audio analysis and sputum smear image classification. Existing studies demonstrate that audio-based models using convolutional neural networks can effectively extract respiratory patterns associated with TB, while image-based CADx systems achieve strong performance in detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis from microscopic sputum samples. In addition, multimodal learning approaches in related works have shown improved diagnostic accuracy when combining cough audio with clinical metadata or imaging data with patient information, highlighting the value of integrating multiple data sources for more robust prediction.
However, despite these advancements, current multimodal TB systems remain limited in scope, as they primarily integrate audio with non-visual clinical variables or imaging with structured patient data, rather than combining complementary biological signals across different sensing domains. Specifically, there is a lack of studies that integrate respiratory audio signals with sputum smear microscopic image analysis within a unified machine learning framework for TB pre-screening diagnostic support system. Furthermore, most existing systems are either clinically dependent or infrastructure-heavy, limiting their applicability in community-based and resource-constrained environments where early TB detection is most critical.
Therefore, this study positions TBhon as a novel and practical implementation of multimodal TB screening by integrating cough audio analysis and sputum smear image classification into a single mobile-based system using machine learning. By fusing these two complementary diagnostic modalities within a lightweight and deployable framework, TBhon addresses the gap between high-performing laboratory-based models and accessible real-world screening tools. The proposed system aims to provide early TB risk estimation in a non-invasive, scalable, and community-oriented manner, representing a first practical step toward multimodal fusion for TB pre-screening diagnostic support system in mobile health environments.

CHAPTER III PROJECT METHODOLOGY AND SYSTEM PLANNING
3.1 Research Design
The study employed a developmental research design focusing on the creation and evaluation of a mobile-based tuberculosis (TB) pre-screening diagnostic support system called TBhon. Developmental research is appropriate for studies involving the design and assessment of technology-based solutions, particularly those that integrate artificial intelligence and machine learning for practical applications (Richey & Klein, 2007).
This study is primarily concerned with evaluating the effectiveness of a multimodal TB risk screening approach using cough audio and sputum smear image inputs. The evaluation focuses on the system’s ability to generate pre-screening diagnostic support outputs categorized into Low, Moderate, and High TB risk levels. The research design emphasizes system performance assessment rather than algorithmic construction.
The study further evaluates the developed system using machine learning performance metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. These metrics are used to determine the reliability and effectiveness of the proposed TB screening approach in handling real-world variability in respiratory sounds and sputum image data.
Unlike experimental clinical studies, this research does not involve medical diagnosis or patient treatment validation. Instead, it focuses on the development and assessment of a computational pre-screening tool intended to support early TB risk identification in low-resource settings.
3.2 System Development Methodology
The development of TBhon follows the CRISP-DM (Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining) methodology, which provides a structured framework for building machine learning-based systems. This methodology is used to guide the technical implementation of the system, including data processing, model training, and deployment of inference mechanisms.

3.7 Dataset Description and Preparation
The study will utilize publicly available tuberculosis (TB) datasets to support the development and evaluation of the proposed TBhon mobile application. 3.7.4 Dataset Distribution
The multimodal TBhon pipeline treats the two input types under different but transparent partitioning schemes. The sputum smear branch follows a conventional three-way split: microscopy images and their YOLO-style labels are organized into training, validation, and testing directories so the CNN can be trained, monitored on a held-out validation set for checkpoint selection, and finally scored on a separate test set. The cough audio branch relies on the TB-Audio dataset’s publisher-defined cross-validation folds: for each fold, a training manifest (X_train_Fold_k.csv) and a disjoint test manifest (X_test_Fold_k.csv) list WAV filenames and binary tb_status labels. TBhon’s default training configuration uses Fold 0, so all cough samples in that fold are assigned either to the training list or to the held-out test list; the current implementation does not introduce a third validation CSV—epoch-wise “validation” metrics in the training script are computed on that held-out test partition. 3.9 Model Training and Testing Procedure
Model development for TBhon was carried out in PyTorch. Two convolutional neural network branches were trained separately: (1) a cough audio classifier that operates on single-channel log-Mel spectrograms and outputs binary labels interpreted as TB-related vs non-TB cough patterns, and (2) a sputum microscopy classifier that operates on RGB slide images and outputs four classes corresponding to AFB load grades (none, low, moderate, high) derived from bacillus counts in the annotation files. Training iterations adjusted network weights to minimize classification error; testing procedures applied frozen checkpoints to held-out data to estimate generalization before integration into the FastAPI inference layer and the mobile client.
Data preparation followed the partitioning described in §3.7.4. Cough samples were drawn from the TB-Audio corpus using the publisher-provided fold files X_train_Fold_k.csv and X_test_Fold_k.csv (default k = 0). Each row supplies a WAV filename and tb_status label; waveforms were resampled to 16 kHz, cropped or padded to a fixed 6 s window, and transformed into 128-bin Mel spectrograms with configured FFT and hop lengths. Sputum images were read from images/{train,val,test} with matching labels/{train,val,test} text files; labels were aggregated into AFB load grades using the binning rule implemented in the training script (0 bacilli → none; 1–4 → low; 5–14 → moderate; ≥15 → high).
Training proceeded epoch-wise with mini-batch stochastic optimization. Both pathways used AdamW with L2-style weight decay (1×10⁻⁴) and cosine annealing of the learning rate over the scheduled epoch count. The cough model used weighted cross-entropy with inverse-frequency class weights estimated from the training fold; the default learning rate was 3×10⁻⁴. The sputum model used cross-entropy with optional per-class weights (enabled by default) computed from the training-set grade distribution; the default learning rate was 1×10⁻³. Checkpoints were retained according to monitoring splits: the cough script tracked macro-F1 on the publisher test CSV (reported in logs as validation-style metrics), while the sputum script tracked classification accuracy on the validation image folder and then reloaded the best weights for a final test-folder evaluation.
Augmentation was applied during training to reduce overfitting to recording conditions and staining variability. For audio, optional time shifting, additive Gaussian noise on the waveform, SpecAugment-style frequency and time masking on the spectrogram, and per-spectrogram standardization were enabled by default. For microscopy images, optional random resized crop, horizontal and vertical flips, small rotations, and color jitter on brightness, contrast, saturation, and hue were composed before ImageNet mean–std normalization at the configured input resolution (224×224 by default).
Testing and reporting combined quantitative scores with diagnostic artifacts. After training, both pipelines wrote metrics (including accuracy, macro-F1, confusion matrices, and classification reports) to JSON; the cough pathway additionally exported a confusion matrix figure. These outputs support comparison across folds or hyperparameter trials and feed the evaluation subsection on classifier reliability prior to multimodal fusion in the application.

3.9.1 Model Performance Evaluation Metrics
Classifier performance for TBhon was assessed with standard supervised classification metrics that summarize agreement between predicted labels and reference labels on held-out data. This applies to the binary cough model (TB-related vs non-TB pattern) and the four-class sputum model (AFB load grades: none, low, moderate, high). Together, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score quantify overall correctness, tendency toward false alarms, sensitivity to the positive class(es), and a harmonic balance between precision and recall (Sokolova & Lapalme, 2009; Powers, 2011). During training runs, metrics were computed from the confusion matrix and reported together with per-class summaries using the same conventions as in the implementation (scikit-learn).
For binary cough classification, true positives (TP), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN), and true negatives (TN) are defined with respect to the positive class (TB-related cough pattern, label 1). For the multiclass sputum classifier, the same confusion-matrix counts support per-class precision and recall via a one-vs-rest view (each grade treated as the positive class against all others), after which macro-F1 averages class-wise F1 values with equal weight—matching the macro-averaged F1 used in the cough training script’s checkpoint selection.

CHAPTER IV SYSTEM ANALYSIS AND DESIGN
4.1 System Architecture
The proposed TBhon application utilizes a multi-component system architecture integrating mobile computing, machine learning, multimodal classification, and screening record management technologies for AI-assisted tuberculosis (TB) pre-screening diagnostic support. The architecture is designed to support respiratory audio analysis, sputum smear image classification, multimodal risk prediction, pre-screening checklist assessment, and mobile-based screening workflows within accessible healthcare environments.
The system architecture consists of five major components including the user layer, application processing layer, machine learning layer, database management layer, and output layer. These architectural components collectively support the operational workflow of the proposed system from user interaction and data submission up to TB risk classification, result generation, and screening record management.
The user layer serves as the primary interaction interface of the TBhon mobile application. This component allows users to register and log into the system, complete the pre-screening checklist, record or upload cough audio samples, capture or upload sputum smear images, access screening history records, and view generated screening results. The mobile interface is designed to improve accessibility and usability for users requiring preliminary TB screening support through portable mobile technologies.
The application processing layer manages the core system workflow and handles validation and preparation of user-submitted inputs. This layer includes the input validation module, audio processing module, image processing module, result management module, and recommendation generation component. The pre-screening checklist responses are also processed within this layer to support contextual interpretation of screening results and assist in generating screening summaries and recommendations.
The machine learning layer performs the primary AI-assisted TB analysis of the system. This component integrates separate deep learning classification models for respiratory audio analysis and sputum smear image analysis. The audio classification model processes Mel-spectrogram representations of cough recordings to identify TB-related respiratory patterns, while the image classification model analyzes sputum smear microscopy images for tuberculosis-related visual indicators. A multimodal fusion module combines the outputs of both classification models together with checklist-based screening context to generate a unified TB risk classification categorized as low, moderate, or high risk.
The database management layer handles the storage and retrieval of screening-related records and machine learning resources utilized by the system. This component manages user account information, pre-screening and screening history records, model configuration files, dataset references, checklist responses, and generated prediction results. The centralized management of these records supports continuity of screening activities and retrieval of historical screening information within the application.
To support cloud deployment for this application we used Digital Ocean services like the droplets and the database to allow us to run our backend and our machine learning in the cloud for better deployment instead of running things locally.
The output layer is responsible for presenting the final screening outputs generated by the TBhon system. This component displays the unified TB risk classification, pre-screening checklist interpretation, screening result summaries, health guidance recommendations, and notification outputs associated with completed screening procedures. The output layer is intended to provide users with understandable and accessible preliminary screening information while encouraging further medical consultation when necessary.
The integration of these architectural components enables the proposed TBhon application to support practical mobile-based tuberculosis TB pre-screening diagnostic support through multimodal AI-assisted analysis, screening workflow management, and accessible healthcare-oriented decision support functionalities.

4.7 Recommendation System Workflow
TBhon supports community-facing TB screening by combining respiratory sound evidence with optional sputum-microscopy imagery so the user receives one interpretable risk signal instead of two disconnected model outputs. The flow is sequential: the mobile client standardizes capture (cough clips and, when available, a microscopy photo), then offloads inference to a companion FastAPI service that hosts the trained convolutional models. The API returns structured probabilities and class labels, which the app maps to discrete risk bands suited to lay users and field use.
Inputs come from on-device recordings and images from the active session. Cough audio is sent to /predict, where the service performs format normalization, resampling, and log-Mel feature extraction before classification; the route can also return a quality-controlled invalid-audio outcome when authenticity checks fail, prompting re-capture instead of forcing a TB probability. When a sputum image is provided, the client posts it to /predict-phlegm, which returns predicted bacterial load category and confidence-related fields for display and logic.
The processing screen handles network resilience: it can try multiple API base URLs (for example LAN endpoints in a pilot), uses stable file paths for uploads, and, with several cough clips, forms an average TB probability before combining modalities. After cough and sputum paths run, the app merges the audio-derived risk with the microscopy-derived risk, while preserving flags for spoofed or unusable audio and for upload failures, so outcomes stay traceable rather than silently defaulting.
The recommendation system strengthened the fused risk level supports triage and follow-up planning (for example facility-based testing or consultation with a clinician) and does not replace definitive diagnosis or treatment decisions, which remain with qualified providers and national TB programs.

4.10 System Security and Data Management
The Tbhon application combines client-side capture, a centralized API, and a relational database to protect, store, synchronize, and retrieve user screening data and model outputs. The backend persists screening sessions together with uploaded sputum imagery, cough recordings, quality checks, ML inference logs (audio and sputum predictions), structured symptom responses, and aggregated screening results including risk levels and recommendations. Authenticated API access (Bearer token validation) limits which records each client can read or write, so completed screenings and their details remain available only to the account that created them, while administrative or operational policies can be enforced at the API layer. Synchronization is achieved by having the mobile client submit uploads and poll or refetch user-scoped history endpoints, which supports consistent management of screening records across devices that use the same account. Together, centralized persistence, token-based access control, and explicit user ownership of sessions improve the reliability, availability, and confidentiality of tuberculosis screening workflows in a deployed mobile setting.

5.11 Challenge Encountered During Development
The development of the proposed TBhon application involved multiple technical, environmental, and implementation challenges associated with multimodal dataset preparation, model training, IoT integration, cloud deployment, and mobile deployment procedures.
One of the primary challenges involved class imbalance in the cough audio dataset, where non-TB samples substantially outnumbered TB-positive samples. This imbalance initially caused the baseline CNN to predict predominantly non-TB classes. The researchers addressed this through hybrid CNN+GBM ensemble training, class-weighted loss functions, and cross-validation across multiple folds.
Another challenge involved balancing model performance and deployment efficiency. Deploying separate backend and ML inference services across two cloud droplets required careful coordination of API endpoints, environment variables, and tunnel URL management. Cloudflare quick tunnel hostnames rotate on restart, necessitating automated synchronization scripts (tunnel:sync) to update mobile environment configuration.
The researchers also encountered challenges associated with IoT device provisioning, particularly in enabling BLE Wi-Fi credential transfer from the mobile application. Full BLE provisioning requires a native development build (EAS/dev client) and is not supported in Expo Go, limiting end-to-end IoT testing during early development phases.
Additional challenges involved multimodal fusion calibration, sputum dataset size limitations (particularly for the "none" AFB class with only 4 test samples), and maintaining acceptable inference latency when processing three cough clips plus one sputum image per screening session over cellular networks.
Despite these challenges, the integration of Agile development methodologies, iterative testing procedures, hybrid model architectures, and cloud-assisted deployment workflows enabled the researchers to refine the proposed system and improve deployment stability, usability, and screening performance throughout the development lifecycle.

Bench testing verified device-triggered media upload and backend session linkage. Full in-app BLE Wi-Fi provisioning requires a native deployment build. This objective was substantially achieved, with functional IoT upload and capture verified during development and testing.

6.1.2 Second Objective - Data Curation and Preprocessing
The second specific objective was to curate and preprocess data by utilizing publicly available tuberculosis datasets and applying signal processing and image feature extraction techniques to ensure model robustness and consistency.
The researchers utilized the Kaggle TB cough audio dataset and a YOLO-formatted sputum smear microscopy dataset. Cough audio samples were resampled to 16 kHz, clipped or padded to a fixed duration, and converted into log-Mel spectrogram representations.
Additional hand-crafted acoustic features including MFCC, delta and delta-delta coefficients, mel statistics, RMS, and zero-crossing rate were extracted for the gradient boosting component. Sputum smear images were annotated with bounding boxes around individual acid-fast bacilli (class 0), counted per image, and converted into binary AFB labels.
Image preprocessing included resizing to 224×224 pixels, ImageNet normalization, and data augmentation. The curated datasets were partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets. This objective was achieved.

6.1.3 Third Objective - Machine Learning Model Development
The third specific objective was to develop and optimize machine learning models using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to classify TB-related patterns in respiratory audio via Mel-spectrograms and to identify visual indicators in Ziehl–Neelsen stained sputum smears. For cough audio, the researchers implemented a hybrid Mel-spectrogram CNN + Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) classifier with a weighted probability blend and calibrated decision threshold.
The CNN branch was trained for eight (8) epochs on fold 1, after which GBM features were extracted and fused at inference. For sputum analysis, a ResNet18 binary CNN classifier was trained for thirty (30) epochs to distinguish AFB-positive from AFB-negative smear images, with a validation-tuned decision threshold under a minimum sensitivity constraint for AFB-positive detection.

6.1.4 Fourth Objective - Multimodal Risk Scoring System
The fourth specific objective was to implement an integrated multi-modal risk scoring system that fuses acoustic and visual classification outputs to generate real-time risk stratification levels. The researchers implemented a multimodal risk fusion module combining an 11-item symptom checklist, mean cough ML tuberculosis probability across quality-validated clips, and sputum ML probability mapped from binary AFB classifier outputs.

6.10 Summary of Finding
Based on testing, validation, and evaluation, TBhon demonstrated multimodal TB pre-screening capability with cloud-assisted session management. The hybrid cough classifier achieved 75.75% test accuracy and 69.08% macro F1. The binary sputum classifier achieved 94.91% test accuracy and 96.19% AFB-positive sensitivity. Weighted multimodal fusion, DigitalOcean deployment with Cloudflare tunnels, and cross-platform mobile deployment strengthened practical applicability. TBhon operated as pre-screening support only, not medical diagnosis.
CHAPTER 7
7.1 Conclusion
The study successfully developed TBhon, an IoT-enabled mobile-based multimodal tuberculosis pre-screening support system integrating an Expo/React Native mobile application, a Node.js/Express backend with Prisma ORM and DigitalOcean Managed MySQL, a Python/FastAPI machine learning inference service with PyTorch models, optional ESP32-S3 IoT acquisition hardware, and weighted multimodal risk fusion for Low, Moderate, and High analysis stratification. The integration of artificial intelligence, mobile computing, cloud deployment, and IoT-assisted data capture enabled the proposed system to support staff-guided screening workflows involving client intake, an eleven-item symptom checklist, cough audio capture, sputum smear imaging, cloud ML inference, staff review, fused risk disclosure, screening history, and patient QR result claim within community and booth-oriented TB pre-screening environments.
The first specific objective, development of IoT acquisition hardware for respiratory audio and microscopic sputum imaging, was substantially achieved. The researchers designed and implemented an ESP32-S3 module with INMP441 cough audio capture and OV5640 sputum imaging, authenticated REST upload to backend /iot endpoints, and mobile-side IoT health verification. Bench testing confirmed device-triggered media upload and session linkage. Full in-application BLE Wi-Fi provisioning requires a native deployment build and remains a refinement item rather than a blocker to the core screening workflow.
The second specific objective, data curation, and preprocessing was achieved. Publicly available cough audio and YOLO-annotated sputum microscopy datasets were partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets. Cough samples were resampled to 16 kHz, converted to log-Mel spectrograms, and supplemented with hand-crafted acoustic features for the gradient boosting branch. Sputum images were annotated, converted to binary acid-fast bacilli labels, resized to 224×224 pixels, and augmented to improve model robustness.
The third specific objective, machine learning model development was achieved. The hybrid Mel-spectrogram CNN + Gradient Boosting Machine cough classifier (production run 20260531_014419) attained 75.75% test accuracy and 69.08% macro F1 on a held-out test fold (n = 2,606), with strong non-TB recall (88.25%) suitable for ruling out lower-risk cases during session. The ResNet18 binary sputum AFB classifier (production run phlegm_afb_binary_20260531_133949) attained 94.91% test accuracy and 96.19% AFB-positive sensitivity on a held-out test partition (n = 216), reflecting a screening-oriented threshold policy that prioritizes sensitivity over specificity. Both models were deployed through FastAPI endpoints with quality gating for invalid cough audio and unusable smear images.
The fourth specific objective multimodal risk scoring was achieved. The system combined checklist probability, mean cough ML probability across quality-validated clips, and sputum ML probability through weighted log-odds fusion (weights 0.85, 1.00, and 0.70) with clinical safety floors for high-concern symptoms and confident AFB-positive findings. The fused output produced interpretable Low, Moderate, and High analysis bands with disclaimers and referral-oriented guidance, extending practical utility beyond isolated modality scores.
The fifth specific objective, communication architecture was achieved. Two DigitalOcean cloud droplets hosted the backend (PM2, port 4000) and ML API (systemd, port 8000), both exposed through Cloudflare HTTPS tunnels to support reliable mobile uploads over cellular networks. The mobile application communicated with the backend for authentication, sessions, and media persistence and with the ML API for inference, while IoT devices uploaded directly to authenticated backend endpoints. This architecture separated application logic, persistence, and inference responsibilities while maintaining HTTPS accessibility for field deployment.
The sixth specific objective—machine learning model evaluation—was achieved. Standard classification metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, confusion matrices, and training-curve compilations demonstrated acceptable offline performance for a pre-screening prototype. Cough TB recall (47.63%) and sputum specificity (50.00% on only six AFB-negative test samples) indicate that the models support screening prioritization rather than standalone clinical diagnosis. Classification response time was largely within the five-second UAT target under stable connectivity, with occasional delays reported on unstable networks.
The seventh and eighth specific objectives ISO/IEC 25010 software quality assessment and professional user acceptance testing were achieved. Six evaluators representing IT, nursing, medical laboratory science, and clinical instruction roles completed six predefined screening tasks and a twenty-four-item questionnaire mapped to Functional Suitability, Performance Efficiency, Usability, Reliability, Security, and Overall Satisfaction. The ISO/IEC 25010 grand mean was 4.53 (SA), with Functional Suitability (4.60), Usability (4.57), Security (4.94), and Overall Satisfaction (4.67) rated Strongly Agree. Performance Efficiency (4.21) and Reliability (4.38) were rated Agree, reflecting connectivity and rural-deployment concerns raised during evaluation. Overall, UAT performance ratings were Excellent (33.3%), Good (50.0%), and Fair (16.7%).
Functional and inference test cases executed on iPhone 15 and Redmi Note 12 Pro 5G devices confirmed that end-to-end screening workflow cases (TC-01 through TC-06) passed on both platforms and that cough quality gating correctly rejected noise, silence, and replay-like inputs while returning consistent API outputs for valid cough bursts and held-out sputum smear images. These results support the practical deployability of the integrated prototype within booth-operated TB pre-screening workflows.
The utilization of CRISP-DM methodology, agile sprint development, multimodal machine learning, cloud infrastructure, and IoT-assisted capture demonstrated the applicability of engineering-oriented health informatics systems within resource-limited Philippine screening contexts. TBhon was consistently positioned and evaluated as pre-screening support only, not a substitute for GeneXpert, smear microscopy, chest radiography, or clinician-confirmed TB diagnosis. Evaluation was conducted on publicly available datasets and prototype booth conditions; therefore, results represent technical feasibility and user acceptability rather than formal clinical validation.
Overall, the proposed TBhon system successfully achieved the research objectives by providing a practical, accessible, and emerging-technology-driven multimodal TB pre-screening platform that combines symptom checklist assessment, cough and sputum machine learning signals, IoT-assisted capture, cloud-backed session management, and staff-reviewed analysis outputs suitable for community health worker and booth deployment contexts in support of earlier referral and organized TB screening activities.
7.2 Recommendation
Based on the findings, limitations, and user acceptance feedback documented in Chapter VI, the researchers recommend the following improvements for future development, field pilot deployment, and research extension.
First, future developers should implement offline or low-bandwidth operations with local request queuing and synchronization when connectivity is restored. UAT evaluators consistently identified unstable Wi-Fi and rural network conditions as the primary limitation (Item 20, weighted mean 3.83), and this enhancement would improve reliability for barangay health stations and remote screening booths.
Second, the mobile application should include a structured onboarding tutorial, illustrated capture guidance, and clearer ML processing status indicators for first-time booth staff. Evaluators from clinical and medical laboratory science backgrounds noted that non-technical users require additional instructional support before independent operation.
Third, referral outputs should integrate location-based mapping to the nearest accredited TB DOTS clinic, RHU, or GeneXpert-capable facility. Clinical evaluators recommended that screening results explicitly link patients to geographically relevant follow-up pathways rather than generic referral text alone.
Fourth, the cough ML pipeline should be expanded with additional training data including female cough recordings, diverse acoustic environments, and improved quality-gate feedback that highlights rejected segments rather than full replay loops to improve TB-class recall and user trust in audio-based screening signals.
Fifth, the sputum analysis module should be enhanced with on-screen AFB bounding-box visualization, expanded AFB-negative training samples, and prospective field validation using institution-approved clinical smear images to strengthen specificity estimates beyond the limited six-sample negative test partition.
Sixth, the symptom checklist may be extended with additional clinical risk factors suggested during UAT such as comorbid conditions, smoking exposure, and allergy-related cough differentiators while preserving a concise staff-operated workflow suitable for booth throughput.
Seventh, IoT deployment should be completed through native or EAS builds enabling full BLE Wi-Fi provisioning, on-device status LEDs or display feedback, and field troubleshooting guides to reduce setup friction for ESP32-S3 cough and sputum capture modules.
Eighth, a multi-site field pilot with LGU, RHU, or NGO partners should be conducted to measure real screening volume, referral follow-through, cloud inference latency under production load, and long-term model drift using logged prediction data from the deployed FastAPI service.
Ninth, future researchers may integrate supplementary modalities such as chest X-ray CAD interfaces or GeneXpert result logging as optional screening inputs only, maintaining honest pre-screening scope and avoiding presentation of the fused risk score as a standalone diagnostic conclusion.
Finally, multilingual support (e.g., Filipino and Cebuano/Bisaya) for checklist items, learn content, result counseling, and disclaimers is recommended to improve accessibility for diverse clients in Mindanao screening environments and to align with community-oriented tuberculosis elimination initiatives.
The researchers strongly recommend the continuous exploration and integration of emerging technologies within community TB pre-screening and public health monitoring environments to improve multimodal screening efficiency, booth-operated screening capability, and technology-assisted tuberculosis elimination sustainability in resource-limited settings.

APPENDIX C – DATASET ANNOTATION DOCUMENTATION
This appendix presents evidence of dataset annotation, labeling procedures, training environment configuration, and model validation outputs for the TBhon multimodal TB pre-screening system. Sputum smear microscopy images use YOLO-format bounding-box annotations for acid-fast bacilli (AFB). Cough audio recordings use participant-level tb_status labels from the CODA TB Kaggle corpus, converted to log-mel spectrograms for CNN training.
C.1 Annotation Workflow
Sputum annotation workflow: each visible AFB rod in a microscopy field was labeled with a normalized YOLO bounding box (class 0). Per-image AFB counts were derived from label files and mapped to load grades (none, low, moderate, high) for CNN binary classification training.
Cough audio labeling workflow: recordings in the Kaggle ruchikashirsath/tb-audio dataset include tb_status metadata (0 = No-TB, 1 = TB-positive). Audio was resampled to 16 kHz, clipped or padded to a fixed duration, and transformed into log-mel spectrograms for hybrid CNN + gradient-boosted classifier training.

APPENDIX D – DATA AUGMENTATION AND DOCUMENTATION
This appendix demonstrates preprocessing and augmentation procedures applied to the TBhon training datasets. Sputum smear examples (D.1–D.4) are taken from Raw_Sputum_Microscopy_Dataset with YOLO AFB bounding boxes drawn for reference. Cough waveform noise (D.5) uses the same additive Gaussian transform as train_tb_cough_hybrid.py. All parameters below are taken directly from ml (phlegm)/train_phlegm_cnn.py and ml/train_tb_cough_hybrid.py.
D.1 Rotation Samples
RandomRotation(±15°) in train_phlegm_cnn.py simulates slight orientation differences during microscopy capture and mobile camera alignment. Three representative AFB-positive fields show original and ±15° variants.
Figure 1. Sputum rotation augmentation samples (±15°).
D.2 Flipping Samples
RandomHorizontalFlip and RandomVerticalFlip (p=0.5) increase spatial invariance. Microscopy fields have no fixed orientation, so flips are valid label-preserving transforms.
Figure D.2. Sputum horizontal and vertical flip samples.
D.3 Brightness Adjustment Samples
ColorJitter(brightness=0.15, contrast=0.15, saturation=0.15, hue=0.03) in train_phlegm_cnn.py adjusts exposure to mimic variable microscope illumination and mobile camera autoexposure. Brightness samples show the ±15% range.
Figure 3. Sputum brightness adjustment samples (±15%).
D.4 Saturation Adjustment Samples
The same ColorJitter block applies saturation=0.15 (and contrast=0.15, hue=0.03) to simulate differences in Ziehl–Neelsen staining intensity across preparation batches.
Figure 4. Sputum saturation adjustment samples (±15%).
D.5 Gaussian Noise Samples
In train_tb_cough_hybrid.py, _augment_waveform() adds Gaussian noise to the 16 kHz cough waveform before log-mel conversion: x = x + randn * noise_std with noise_std=0.008. Sputum CNN training does not apply pixel-level noise; only ColorJitter and geometric transforms.
Figure 5. Cough waveform Gaussian noise samples
D.6 Augmentaiton Workflow
End-to-end augmentation workflow for both sputum CNN and cough hybrid model training pipelines.
Figure D.6. TBhon multimodal data augmentation workflow.
Sputum training transforms (train_phlegm_cnn.py): RandomResizedCrop(224, scale=(0.85, 1.0), ratio=(0.9, 1.1)), RandomHorizontalFlip(0.5), RandomVerticalFlip(0.5), RandomRotation(15), ColorJitter(brightness=0.15, contrast=0.15, saturation=0.15, hue=0.03), ImageNet normalization.
